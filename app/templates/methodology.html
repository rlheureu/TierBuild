Methodology

The aesthetic is the hook; the methodology is the moat. No opaque percentages.

How tiers are earned

Each tier is anchored to a performance target — a resolution + framerate a component can sustain in demanding current AAA titles at high settings, native resolution, no upscaling. A component earns a tier if it meets the target in the median title of a rotating basket of ~10 demanding games from the last three years, refreshed twice yearly. Not the best case, not the pathological worst case.

Resolution loads the GPU; framerate loads the CPU

GPU load scales with resolution (and with the framerate the panel demands). CPU load scales primarily with framerate. So a CPU's tier is set by the framerate column of its target only. That's why a Rare CPU with a Legendary GPU is a real bottleneck for a 1440p @ 144 gamer and a perfectly balanced build for a 4K @ 60 gamer — and why we always ask for both resolution and framerate.

Your monitor is the question

Gamers think “I have a 1440p 144Hz monitor,” not “I am Epic tier.” The loot tier is the answer; resolution and framerate are the question. Every build check shows a concrete res @ fps target line so the bottleneck is always relative to the panel you actually use.

Modifiers, not fudge factors

Upscaling (DLSS/FSR/XeSS) appears as a separate badge and is never folded into a base tier. Frame generation is excluded from tier math entirely in v1. GPUs with insufficient VRAM for their raster tier carry a visible warning.

Tier drift is honest

Tiers are re-benchmarked against the current AAA basket, so hardware can move down a tier as games get heavier. That's not a rug-pull — it's what actually happens to aging hardware. Every recalibration should ship with a changelog explaining every move.

About recommendations

Upgrade recommendations are deterministic from the tier math: the cheapest single swap that brings your build to your resolution × framerate target. Affiliate links never change which part gets recommended.
